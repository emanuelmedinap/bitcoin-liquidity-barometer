System liquidity vs. bank Net Interest Income

Does system liquidity — the money supply, policy rates, and the yield-curve slope — drive US bank net interest income (NII), with bitcoin a barometer of that liquidity rather than a cause of bank revenue?

Method. A quarter-aligned panel of US public banks (SEC EDGAR XBRL) joined to FRED liquidity series (M2, fed funds, yield slope) and bitcoin; modeled in growth/changes rather than levels, with a temporal split and no imputation.

Findings

Answering our research question: No, the money-supply dimension of system liquidity (M2) does not drive US bank NII. Across both historical episodes, 2020 to 2021 (COVID: the Fed printing money) and 2022 to 2023 (the Fed making money disappear), I found that the two series moved in opposite directions, and that both movements trace to a single common cause: the policy rate. I therefore reject the liquidity drives NII hypothesis and reserve causal language for the one channel with a documented mechanism, interest rates → NII (Borio, Gambacorta & Hofmann, 2017). Nevertheless, bitcoin behaves exactly as proposed: a barometer of the M2 flood of money, tracking its direction with amplified magnitude in both episodes, a claim of co-movement, never causation. To support this conclusion as an economics enthusiast, here are the details of the analysis:

  • Inference window. Panel B only, 2018 Q1 → 2024 Q2 (26 quarters), read in year over year changes rather than levels, since two trending series can look related by accident (Granger & Newbold, 1974).
    • Evidence 1:
      • 2022 to 2023, the drain (QT). NII growth spiked ~+30 percentage points and crossed above M2, while M2 growth fell and sat at zero, the first contraction in decades. If M2 wasn't pushing NII up, what was?
        • The Fed: asset purchases stopped and rates rose aggressively, a well documented, one time historical event. Barometer check: bitcoin followed M2's direction with amplified magnitude (~-65% in the 2022 drain vs. M2's ~-4.6%).
    • Evidence 2:
      • 2020 to 2021, the flood (COVID). M2 growth skyrocketed to ~+30 pp (two slopes: a burst, then a plateau), while NII growth spent the entire period in negative territory (four slopes). The loudest liquidity event in the sample, yet bank interest income fell, because rates sat at zero. Barometer check: bitcoin surged with the flood, again exaggerating M2's direction.

Both episodes are historic, first of their kind events, which is why I treat them as strong evidence of divergence, not as a settled law.

Caveats & disclaimer

A caveat on where I stand: I come to this as an enthusiast of these topics, not a finance professional. My conclusions should be read as an analyst's exploration, not an expert's verdict.

  • FY2025 figures are unverified. The most recent quarters rely on the filed XBRL facts themselves; I have not independently re-confirmed the published FY2025 10-K. Those rows are explicitly labeled UNVERIFIED.
  • The macro story is low-N. There are only ~48 quarters of history; cross-bank breadth is large, but the time-series evidence on liquidity is thin — treat macro conclusions as suggestive, not settled.
  • Correlation is not causation. Causal language is reserved for the one channel with a clear mechanism — interest rates → NII. For bitcoin the claim is co-movement only: a barometer of the same weather, not the cause of it.
  • The system-NII aggregate is thin-coverage before ~2018. The system series is a sum of NII across banks, and coverage ramps over time — the early quarters run over as few as 16 banks in 2008, ramping to ~167 from 2020 on. So its early year-over-year swings are composition artifacts, not economics. Inference is reserved for the stable-coverage period, and the thin window is shaded in the figure above.

References

Anchor papers — the two studies the design leans on (DECISIONS.md §2):

  • Borio, Gambacorta & Hofmann (2017), The influence of monetary policy on bank profitability. A panel of 109 large international banks, 1995–2012: both the level of short-term rates and the slope of the yield curve raise bank profitability, operating primarily through net interest income (M2 is this study's own extension, not one of their variables).
  • Granger & Newbold (1974), Spurious regressions in econometrics. Regressing one independent random walk on another routinely yields "significant" but entirely spurious results — the reason this study models in changes, not levels.
